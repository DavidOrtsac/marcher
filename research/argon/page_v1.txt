===SUMMARY===
MARCHER is a hyperlocal map for planning and walking one EVA at Curiosity's boxwork on Mount Sharp. Existing Mars maps serve regional and global views, while MARCHER works on one 3.2 km square. It fuses HiRISE 1 m terrain and 25 cm imagery, CRISM minerals, THEMIS thermal inertia, Curiosity's stops and photos, REMS weather, RAD radiation and DONKI space weather into one web tool. The planner routes a suited astronaut from a crew rover across six real Curiosity stops on the least metabolic energy path that stays under a 20° slope. It computes a live GO, CAUTION or NO-GO verdict from energy, oxygen, radiation dose, daylight, walk-back time, radio line of sight, weather and space weather. In walk mode, a visor display guides the astronaut along the route, and Curiosity's ground photos hang in 3D where its cameras stood so the crew sees the real rocks before stepping outside. The title screen offers two ways in. Start Demo is a short guided walk that explains the site, the science goal, a solar storm drill and the safety checks in plain words. Mission Control is the full habitat planner, laid out like desktop mapping software with menus, layers, a timeline and GeoJSON and CSV export. MARCHER matters because the first human crews will plan EVAs around targets that robots have already found, and it proves that the orbital and surface data needed to plan and walk those routes already sits in NASA archives.
===DETAILS===
**The problem and the benefit.** Mars maps today are built for regional and global views. Human crews on the surface will need maps built at the scale of a single walk. The first astronauts on Mars will plan EVAs around science targets that robotic missions such as Curiosity have already found and studied. MARCHER answers the Martian Map challenge by fusing orbital imagery, orbital spectroscopy, rover surface photos, surface weather, radiation records and live space weather into one browser tool for planning and walking one EVA. It shows that the data needed to plan a safe, scientifically rich walk already sits in NASA archives, and that orbital and surface records can work together before and during an EVA.

**The site and the science goal.** MARCHER opens on a 3.2 km square of Mount Sharp in Gale Crater, where Curiosity studied boxwork ridges from mid 2025 to March 2026. The built-in EVA plan, called "Ridge and hollow transect", starts from a crew rover parked at Altadena, which was Curiosity's first boxwork drill site. The route visits six places where Curiosity stopped to study the boxwork: Volcán Peña Blanca, Salar de Agua Amara, the Río Frío wall, the Monte Grande hollow, the Nevado Sajama ridge and Timboy Chaco. Two of those six stops are Curiosity drill sites. The science goal of the walk is to take a rock sample from a ridge and another from the hollow beside it, matching the pair Curiosity drilled about 10 m apart at Valle de la Luna in the hollow and Nevado Sajama on the ridge, to test how ancient groundwater cemented the ridges. Curiosity drove from Altadena to Timboy Chaco over 267 sols, from sol 4560 to sol 4827, and its real drive replays on the map. In MARCHER, the astronaut visits the same places on foot in 4 h 09.

**Which NASA data does which job.** Every visual layer, safety check and science readout in MARCHER traces directly to a specific NASA dataset, model or standard:
- HiRISE 1 m stereo terrain builds the elevation mesh, sets the 2 m routing grid, measures slope and short walls, computes the radio viewshed and maps boxwork ridges and hollows using band-pass relief.
- HiRISE 25 cm imagery drapes over the 3D terrain mesh to show the ground surface.
- CRISM spectroscopy maps hydrated sulfates on the ground and feeds the visor ground tags.
- THEMIS infrared data maps thermal inertia across the 3.2 km square.
- Curiosity's route and stop records place the six science waypoints and replay its 267-sol drive from Altadena to Timboy Chaco.
- Curiosity's Navcam and Mastcam photos, positioned using their CAHVOR camera models and Curiosity's attitude, hang in 3D at each stop so the astronaut sees rocks too small for 1 m orbital terrain to resolve.
- Curiosity's REMS daily reports provide sunrise and sunset checks for the clock and build the seasonal weather outlook from the median of earlier Mars years.
- Curiosity's RAD surface measurements set the baseline radiation dose rate of 0.64 mSv per day at Gale Crater and provide the 2 to 3 times dose rate rise from the September 2017 event for the solar storm drill.
- NASA DONKI is read live when the map loads to check for coronal mass ejections predicted to reach Mars within a day of the planned EVA.
- NASA 3D models render the astronaut as the Z-2 suit prototype and the shelter as the Space Exploration Vehicle crew rover.
- NASA human spaceflight standards and studies anchor the planner and visor, including the 20° maximum walking slope from the Artemis EVA-EXP-0075 concept of operations, the 2100 BTU/hr suited metabolic cap from NASA-STD-3001, oxygen use from NASA BVAD, suit and crew masses and the 8 hour EVA budget at 1200 BTU/hr from ICES-2026-127, Apollo 14 slope speed data, the Mars24 Sun clock algorithm and the Joint Augmented Reality Visual Informatics System suit display design from NASA Johnson (CTSD-ADV-1788 Rev A and ICES-2023-298).

**Start Demo: for first-time visitors.** The title screen offers two ways into MARCHER. Start Demo is a short guided game built for someone who has never seen a Mars map. It opens on a 3D globe of Mars that turns to Gale Crater and pins the site, stating the objective before the walk begins. You tap once to walk the real route from the crew rover at the planned pace of 2 km/h and tap again to stop, viewing the astronaut from behind or through the helmet. A speed button steps through ×1, ×5, ×15 and ×30. Whenever a voice speaks, the speed drops back to ×1 so every spoken line is heard at the exact place it describes. The full demo takes about five and a half minutes at ×30. A crewmate inside the crew rover talks you through the walk, while the suit computer speaks for the first rock sample and the solar storm drill. As you walk, an objective list strikes itself out: you collect six rock samples, answer a solar storm drill and walk home. During the drill, which is clearly marked as a drill, a red ghost astronaut walks the fastest safe line back to the crew rover, a shorter path than the outbound route. A side list slides open to jump to any stop on the route or open Mission Control. Start Demo runs on the live planner, so the clock, distances, radio link and escape route are all real numbers computed for that route.

**The suit display and walk mode.** Walk mode acts as the astronaut's visor. As Start Demo progresses, a head-up display builds itself in the upper right of the view one instrument at a time, appearing on the exact sentence where the crewmate introduces it. Effort shows metabolic rate in watts against a tick at the 615 W cooling limit. Energy shows the share of the EVA budget used so far, marked where the walk will finish at 43%. Shelter shows walk-back minutes on foot to the crew rover. Steep ground brings up grade in degrees against the 20° limit, and the solar storm drill adds cumulative radiation dose. From the third rock sample onward, ground tags name ridges or hollows with their local relief from the 1 m terrain, or hydrated minerals from CRISM. Following NASA Johnson's Joint Augmented Reality Visual Informatics System prototype, the display uses one colour, sits right of centre above the horizon and has no controls to operate. Characters are not a font file. Each letter or number is drawn stroke by stroke using straight lines of one pen width. The display lags slightly behind head turns, sways with footsteps and jolts on the storm alarm so it reads like helmet hardware. Full walk mode adds a heading tape marking stops, the crew rover and the Sun, plus readouts for oxygen used, metabolic rate, walk-back time, radio link, grade and Sun elevation. In third-person view, the Z-2 suit figure walks with a stride driven by ground covered and casts its real silhouette shadow from the computed Sun position. At each stop, the camera moves to where Curiosity's cameras stood and hangs Navcam and Mastcam photos in 3D along their real camera rays. Testing Navcam pointing against the HiRISE terrain skyline showed azimuth agreement of 0.3 ± 0.6°. For 3D display only, photo brightness is matched to neighbours and the sky is faded out, while the side panel always shows the untouched NASA image. Triggering the solar storm drill mid-walk switches the visor to the fastest return path, counts down walk-back minutes and shows the September 2017 RAD dose rise for context.

**Mission Control: the habitat planner.** Mission Control opens from the title screen, the demo side list or the end of the walk. On a desktop, it is laid out like standard mapping software with a menu bar (File, Edit, View, Tools, Help), a toolbar, a Layers panel with checkboxes on the left, a plan panel on the right, a bottom timeline and a status bar reading map scale, camera height and pointer latitude, longitude, elevation and slope. Map controls include a compass, a 2D and 3D switch and zoom buttons, while phones get a simpler touch layout. Users can toggle terrain overlays for slope and short walls, walk-back contours, radio line of sight, boxwork ridges and hollows, Curiosity's drive, THEMIS thermal inertia and CRISM hydrated sulfates. Band-pass relief on the 1 m terrain matches the Curiosity team's stop labels, showing the Monte Grande hollow about 0.3 m below its surroundings and the Nevado Sajama ridge about 0.3 m above. Clicking any point reads every layer there, identifies the nearest opposite feature to form a ridge and hollow rock sample pair and lets the user add that spot as a stop. Users can also remove added stops, step to the previous or next sol, trigger the solar storm drill from the timeline, reset the plan and export the route and stops as GeoJSON or the stops as CSV. Four menu items are marked Soon and are not built yet: Import plan, Measure distance, Second astronaut and Crew rover leg. Every other menu item and button works.

**Live routing and safety verdicts.** Every route schedule and GO, CAUTION or NO-GO verdict is computed live. Routing finds the path of least metabolic energy across a 2 m grid using A* search in 16 directions. It blocks slopes above 20° and adds two rules of our own: any 1 m step steeper than 30° acts as a wall, and side slopes past 8° add 4% energy cost per degree. Energy and walking speed use the MIT SEXTANT traverse model (Johnson 2010), built on the Santee et al. 2001 load carriage model and Apollo 14 slope speeds. Walking speed caps at 2 km/h and slows on climbs to stay below 2100 BTU/hr. Our walker combines three masses from ICES-2026-127: an 82 kg crew member, a 104 kg Mars suit and 22.7 kg of tools and samples. Walk-back time from every point on the map is computed with Dijkstra's algorithm using the same energy and speed rules. If DONKI shows a CME arriving within one day of the EVA, the verdict shifts to CAUTION. For the default six-stop plan, the walk covers 1.42 km in 4 h 09 including stop times, climbs 64 m with a 15.1° maximum grade, uses 43% of the energy budget, consumes 0.31 kg of oxygen and adds 0.11 mSv of radiation. Walk-back time never exceeds 17 minutes, 6.3 hours of daylight remain at the end, radio line of sight holds along the entire route and the verdict is GO.

**Known limits and what MARCHER hopes to achieve.** MARCHER documents its physical and data limits clearly:
- Orbital terrain at 1 m cannot resolve individual rocks, and 25 cm imagery looks soft up close because nothing synthetic is added to sharpen it, which is why Curiosity's ground photos are hung at the stops.
- The walking energy model comes from Earth and Apollo data because suited walking in Mars gravity is untested, and the 20° slope limit and 2 km/h pace come from lunar EVA planning.
- The public REMS outreach feed trails Curiosity by about 80 sols, while the full science record sits in the PDS.
- Radio checks test geometric line of sight from a 3 m crew rover mast to a 1.9 m helmet with no link budget, ignoring ground intrusions smaller than the first Fresnel zone at 415 MHz, the orbital spacesuit EVA voice frequency in Report ITU-R SA.2162.
- DONKI tracks CMEs already launched and cannot predict unlaunched solar storms, which is why walk mode includes the solar storm drill.
Next, the project aims to support any site with HiRISE stereo terrain, live suit telemetry on the visor and two-person EVAs with a crew rover in reserve.

**Tools, languages, hardware and software.** All of the software is new and was written during the hackathon on November 14 to 15, 2026. The web application is built in JavaScript using ES modules and three.js for 3D rendering, running as a static app in any modern desktop or phone browser. A browser web worker runs the 16-direction A* router, the Dijkstra walk-back field and the radio viewshed. Offline data baking uses Python with GDAL, NumPy and Pillow. Automated testing covers 108 tests across the Mars clock and Sun math, weather outlook and space weather logic. The demo video was captured frame by frame from the live app using Playwright and encoded with FFmpeg. The source code is released under the Apache 2.0 license at https://github.com/DavidOrtsac/marcher.
